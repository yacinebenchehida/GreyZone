01Read the interfaceControls, plots and the number to watch

Choose your settings, then press Run simulation. Pause freezes the run; +1 generation advances once and stays paused. Changing population or sampling settings resets the run. Run again starts a fresh random history.

The ocean
The counter tracks generations since separation. Basin labels give each population’s effective size (Ne); animal counts are compressed above 200. Animal choice and travel speed change the illustration. Generation speed changes playback, not the model per generation.
The FST plot
The solid teal line is differentiation in the full simulated populations, using all selected SNPs. The band spans the central 95% of sampled FST estimates from the repeated studies. It shows sampling variation for this history, not variation among different evolutionary histories.
Power and the large number
The blue curve is the fraction of studies detecting differentiation. The dashed line marks 80%. Exit is confirmed after five consecutive generations at ≥80% power; the large number reports the first of those five. A dip resets an unconfirmed sequence. Later dips remain possible; detection does not stop the run.
SNPs and individuals
A/B bars show population allele frequencies. Show SNPs displays SNPs 1–10 on animals. Animal close-up shows an individual’s 0, 1 or 2 copies of allele A. Gold means still segregating; grey means shared fixation; turquoise/coral mean opposite fixation.
Your sample

02Understand the grey zoneSeparation, genetic differences and the evidence for them

One population splits into two with identical starting allele frequencies. At m = 0, no gene flow continues. At m > 0, the channel allows exchange. Genetic drift changes frequencies by chance; migration mixes the gene pools. A barrier does not create an immediate genetic difference.

FST: how different are the gene pools?
FST summarises differences in allele frequencies. Near 0 means similar frequencies; higher values mean stronger differentiation. Near 1 means little variation within basins relative to variation between them. Some SNPs can still be fixed for the same allele on both sides.
Power: how reliably would a study detect it?
At each generation, 1,000 studies use your chosen samples and SNPs. Each tests for allele-frequency differences at p ≤ 0.05. Power of 80% means about 800 studies detect differentiation. At the identical starting state, occasional significant results occur by chance.
The grey zone: separation that is hard to detect
Populations may become demographically independent before neutral genetic differences are readily detectable. Here, the grey shading tracks the period before the chosen study reaches the five-generation power criterion. Its duration depends on the scenario and study design.
A small FST can still be significant
FST measures the magnitude of differentiation; power describes detection with the available data. There is no universal FST value at which every study succeeds. Sampled FST can also be slightly negative through sampling noise.

No detected difference does not establish demographic connectivity. It can reflect ongoing gene flow, a recent split, slow drift, or limited sampling. Equally, a significant genetic difference alone does not establish demographic independence or define a conservation unit.

03Know what each parameter changesPopulation processes and study design
Population parameters shape evolution; sampling parameters shape what a study can detect.
SettingHow to interpret it
Ancestral NeEffective population size controls drift; it need not equal the census count in nature. Smaller populations generally drift faster. This control is the total before the split: Ne = 2,000 with a 50:50 split gives Ne = 1,000 per basin.
Share going westDivides the ancestral total between the basins. An unequal split gives different drift strengths; the smaller population generally changes faster.
Migration mThe fraction of each receiving population’s gene pool coming from the other per generation. m = 0.001 means 0.1%, not one migrant. More exchange generally keeps frequencies more similar and can delay detection.
Sampled from each sideThe number studied per basin, distinct from Ne. More individuals generally improve precision and power, with diminishing returns. Sampling is capped at the smaller population size.
Independent neutral markersThe SNPs used in every study. More informative, independent SNPs generally improve detection. Every selected SNP enters the calculation; only the first ten appear on animals. These markers do not represent adaptation.

Read Ne and m together. In a basin with Ne = 1,000, m = 0.001 gives an expected effective migrant contribution of Ne × m = 1 per generation. With Ne = 10,000, the same fraction gives 10. Ne × m helps interpret mixing relative to drift; it is not a universal threshold for independence.

04Try four comparisonsChange one setting, predict the pattern, then watch

Start with ancestral Ne 2,000 · 50:50 split · m = 0 · 40 sampled per side · 50 SNPs. Use this as a reference for each comparison.

  1. Make populations larger

    Compare ancestral Ne 2,000 and 20,000. Watch for slower differentiation and a longer grey zone on average in larger populations.

  2. Open the channel

    Compare m = 0, 0.001 and 0.01. More mixing can keep FST low and postpone detection. The target may remain unreached during your run. Then try the same m values at larger Ne.

  3. Collect more individuals

    Compare 10 and 80 sampled per side, keeping the other settings. Expect generally higher power and less sampling spread with larger samples.

  4. Genotype more SNPs

    Compare 10 and 100 SNPs, with 40 individuals per side. More independent, informative markers can reveal subtle differentiation. Adding data improves detection; it does not accelerate the populations’ evolution.

Balance samples and markers. Many SNPs do not automatically compensate for very few individuals. Try increasing both.

Repeat each setting. Every run follows a new random history, including after a setting changes. Compare patterns across several runs, not just two crossing numbers. A run with no confirmed exit is also informative.

05Use the result in contextAn illustration and the question it helps you ask

The conservation question is: under an assumed separation, population history and study design, how many generations are needed to detect differentiation reliably? A generation is a reproductive timescale; converting it to years requires the real species’ generation time. Changing the animal artwork does not change that timescale.

For a real system, compare the time since a suspected separation with detection lags across plausible histories. If those histories predict high power by that time, continued non-detection weighs against them. If power should still be low, a non-significant result cannot distinguish the alternatives well.

This animation illustrates one simplified history with constant population sizes, neutral unlinked SNPs and no mutation. It does not estimate demographic independence. Its 1,000 resamples describe repeated studies of one evolving population pair, not 1,000 evolutionary histories.

The planned R package will provide the more flexible framework for demographic changes, mutation, marker properties and multiple evolutionary histories, to assess how consistent the results are. The generation shown here is conditional on this run and its assumptions.

+Model detailsAssumptions, sampling and calculation methods

This simulation uses two neutral diploid Wright–Fisher populations with constant sizes and unlinked biallelic markers. Their sizes add to the ancestral total, and both start with exactly matching allele frequencies. No selection, mutation, sex structure or spatial effects are included. The animals represent the simulated populations. Animal selection changes their appearance; the same neutral population model is used for every choice.

Migration is the proportion m of each receiving population’s gene pool replaced per generation, with 0 ≤ m < 1. Both populations use the same m. Before drift, frequencies become (1 − m)p₁ + mp₂ in the west, and (1 − m)p₂ + mp₁ in the east. Expected migrant contributions are mNe for each receiving population, so they can differ when population sizes differ. The few animated crossings illustrate exchange intensity; they are not a migrant census.

FST is Hudson’s ratio of summed diversities. Samples use independent gene-copy draws and an approximate multilocus Pearson chi-square test at α = 0.05. Rare alleles and small samples can affect calibration. Each Run generates one live population history. At every generation, 1,000 independent study datasets are sampled from that exact population pair, using the selected individuals per basin and all selected SNPs. Each dataset supplies a sampled FST and a chi-square test. The fraction of significant studies estimates power for the populations you see. Grey-zone exit is confirmed only after five consecutive generations with detection power at or above 80%. The central number reports the first generation of the earliest qualifying sequence, confirmed four generations later. Any dip below 80% before confirmation resets the sequence. This is a five-generation stability rule, not a guarantee that power will remain above 80% forever. Evolution continues after confirmation, until you pause it or all markers are stably fixed. With no migration, fixation within both basins is stable; with migration, opposite fixation can be temporary, so automatic completion requires shared fixation at every marker. If fixation occurs during an unconfirmed sequence, sampling continues for up to four further generations to finish the check. There is no fixed generation cutoff. Pause freezes generations, animals, water and study calculation. Switching tabs does not pause the run, although browsers may throttle background computation.

“Show SNPs” displays SNPs 1–10, or all selected SNPs if fewer than ten. Gold indicates a locus still segregating within at least one basin: empty = 0 copies of allele A (B/B), half-filled = 1 (A/B), filled = 2 (A/A). Animal close-up shows the same genotypes as readable numbers labelled SNP 1, SNP 2, and so on. With ten markers, SNPs 1–5 run from head towards tail on the animal’s left side, and SNPs 6–10 do the same on its right side. With five or fewer, they run along the centreline. Left and right refer to the animal’s own sides; the arrangement rotates as it swims. Grey indicates the same allele fixed in both basins; turquoise in the west and coral in the east indicate different alleles fixed. The All SNPs panel lists every marker with paired population-frequency bars and an optional segregating-only filter. Segregating bars show allele A in gold and allele B in pale gold, with both percentages. At shared fixation both bars are solid grey; at opposite fixation the west bar is solid turquoise and the east bar solid coral. Each fixed bar names the allele, A or B, so a zero frequency of A never appears as an empty grey bar. Colours change when the marker state changes. The actual genotypes and original allele-A frequencies are unchanged. The dots use stable gene-copy ranks within a run, so unchanged allele frequencies keep the same display rather than reshuffling colours each generation. They are frequency illustrations, not individual pedigrees. Significance does not freeze evolution. Fixed genotypes stay fixed unless an allele is reintroduced; grey always means shared fixation, while light gold/orange means variation remains within at least one basin. FST near 1 indicates little variation within populations; some fixed SNPs can still carry the same allele on both sides, while others fix opposite alleles. The dots follow the model allele frequencies at each generation update, rounded to the displayed headcount. The statistical test always uses all selected markers. Body colour does not encode genetic identity or adaptation.

All calculations run in your browser. The power analysis runs separately so the animals can keep swimming.
