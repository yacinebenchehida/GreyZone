01Find your way aroundA visual guide to the controls and results

Choose your settings, then press Run simulation. Land separates the population into two basins. Watch generations pass and see when genetic sampling can reliably tell the populations apart.

  1. Set the scenario and your study. The dock below the ocean contains population size, split ratio, migration, sampled individuals and SNP count. The Settings link at the top takes you straight there. Save & reproduce contains the seed and settings files.
  1. Follow the generations. The counter is time since the split. Basin labels show Ne on each side. The demographic state appears above the ocean.
  2. Run, pause, inspect. Pause freezes the simulation. +1 generation advances once and stays paused. Changing a population or sampling setting resets the run; Run again starts another random history unless Reuse this seed is selected.
  1. The number to watch. Generations needed to leave the grey zone. It appears once power stays at or above 80% for five consecutive generations, and reports the first of those five. A brief crossing is not enough. The run continues afterwards.
  2. How different are they? The teal line is FST in the full populations. The shaded band contains the middle 95% of sampled FST estimates: wider means a study’s estimate is less precise.
  3. How reliably can we detect it? The blue line is power. The dashed line is the 80% target; the grey shading marks the time before confirmed exit.

Explore the genetics. Up to 100 SNPs, All SNPs shows allele frequencies in both basins. Above 100, SNP summary shows counts of segregating, differently fixed and identically fixed markers, with no individual bars. Every selected SNP is included in the calculations. Turn on Show SNPs to see the first ten on animals; click an animal for its 0, 1 or 2 copies of allele A. Gold means still variable, grey means the same allele fixed on both sides, and turquoise/coral mean different alleles fixed.

Save an experiment. Open Save & reproduce in the settings. Save a settings file or copy a link to reopen the same history from generation 0. Select Reuse this seed to repeat it; New seed chooses a different history.

Try being the researcher. Pause, then click Take a genetic sample a few times. The populations stay the same, but samples can give different answers.

02What is the grey zone?Separation comes first; detectable differences can take time

Before separation, the two regions form one connected population. Individuals move between them, so they are not demographically independent.

After complete separation (m = 0), each population depends on its own reproduction and survival rather than immigration from the other. They are demographically independent, but initially still have identical allele frequencies.

Genetic drift gradually creates differences. Those differences may remain too small for a study to detect reliably. This delay between demographic independence and detectable genetic differentiation is the grey zone. Its duration depends on how populations evolve and how we sample them.

When migration continues, the populations remain partly connected. Limited exchange can maintain genetic similarity even when their demographic dynamics are largely independent. A small migration fraction alone does not establish demographic independence.

FST measures differentiation
Near 0, the gene pools have similar allele frequencies. Higher values mean stronger differentiation. At 1, variation within populations is absent while some SNPs differ between them; other SNPs may be fixed identically.
Power measures detection
Each generation, 1,000 studies sample the populations using your chosen individuals and SNPs. Power of 80% means about 800 detect a significant difference. It describes repeated studies, not how confident one particular result is.
The grey zone is the detection lag
After isolation, populations can be demographically independent while their neutral genetic differences remain difficult to detect. Here we track the wait until the five-generation 80% power criterion is met.
Small differences can be detectable
There is no universal FST threshold for significance. With enough data, even a small difference can be detected reliably. Differentiation and the ability to detect it are different quantities.

How does a study detect differentiation?

Each study compares allele counts between the populations using an approximate Pearson chi-square test, combining evidence across the independent SNPs. It asks whether the differences are larger than expected from sampling alone if the populations had identical allele frequencies. A study detects a significant difference when p ≤ 0.05.

FST measures the amount of differentiation; the test assesses the evidence for a difference. Power is the percentage of the 1,000 studies obtaining a significant result.

A barrier need not produce an immediate genetic signal. A non-significant result does not prove populations are connected. Conversely, a significant difference alone does not prove demographic independence.

03What do the settings change?Drift, migration and the data you collect
Ne and m shape the populations; samples and SNPs shape your ability to tell them apart.
SettingWhat to watch
Ancestral NeEffective population size: smaller populations experience stronger random drift. This is the total before the split: 100 at 50:50 gives 50 per basin; 50,000 gives 25,000 per basin. Ne need not equal the number of animals counted in nature.
Share going westHow the total is divided. An unequal split makes drift stronger on the smaller side.
Migration mThe fraction of each basin’s gene pool coming from the other each generation. 0 = no gene flow; 0.01 = 1%. More exchange generally keeps frequencies closer.
Sampled from each sideIndividuals in each study, rather than the whole population. More individuals generally give more precise estimates and greater power. The smaller basin limits the sample size.
Independent neutral markersSNPs analysed in each study. More informative, independent markers generally increase power. These markers track neutral genetic variation, not adaptation.

Ne and m work together. At the same migration fraction, drift is weaker in a large population, so migration can more easily keep the gene pools similar. The same m can therefore give very different outcomes at different Ne.

04Try it: four lessons to discoverChange one setting and compare what happens

  1. Tiny versus large populations

    Compare ancestral Ne = 100 and 50,000. Use a 50:50 split, m = 0, 20 sampled per side and 20 SNPs. Pause each run around generation 50 and compare FST and power. In the tiny populations, drift should create differences much faster; large populations can stay genetically similar long after isolation. Then continue and watch the detection lag.

  2. Let migrants through

    Set ancestral Ne = 1,000, 50:50, 20 sampled per side and 20 SNPs. Compare m = 0, 0.01 and 0.05. Watch how mixing can hold FST down and delay detection. Try those same fractions at Ne = 50,000: weaker drift makes gene flow harder to overcome. The 80% target may remain unreached.

  3. Collect more individuals

    Use ancestral Ne = 1,000, 50:50, m = 0 and 20 SNPs. Compare 5 and 100 sampled per side. Larger samples generally narrow the FST band and reach high power sooner. Collecting more data helps reveal divergence; it does not make the populations diverge faster.

  4. Read more of the genome

    Keep ancestral Ne = 1,000, 50:50 and m = 0, with 20 sampled per side. Compare 5 and 100 SNPs. More independent markers generally improve detection. Then reduce the sample to 5: many SNPs do not always compensate for very few individuals.

Repeat, rather than expect an exact number. Every run has a new random history. Compare the overall patterns across several runs. For large populations, you can see the lesson before reaching fixation—or even the power target.

The question to take away: given this population size, gene flow and study design, how long could isolation remain genetically hard to detect?

+How are the studies calculated?A short note on the model and significance test

The simulator follows one pair of neutral diploid populations with constant sizes, independent biallelic SNPs and no mutation. Migration mixes allele frequencies before random drift each generation.

Each of the 1,000 studies draws the selected sample size from each basin and uses all selected SNPs. Sampled FST uses Hudson’s estimator; significance uses an approximate Pearson chi-square test of allele counts at p ≤ 0.05. This is not a permutation test of FST. Power is the fraction of significant studies. Sampling noise can give slightly negative FST estimates, and occasional significant results even at the identical starting state. Very small samples and rare alleles can make the approximate test less reliable.

The FST band spans the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles of those studies. It describes sampling variation around this population history, not the range of possible evolutionary histories.
